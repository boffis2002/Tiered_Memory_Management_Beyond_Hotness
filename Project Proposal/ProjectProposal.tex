\begin{abstract}
Tiered memory systems combine a fast tier (local DRAM) with a larger but slower
tier (e.g., CXL-attached memory) and typically decide data placement based on
access frequency (``hotness''). Liu et al.~\cite{liu2025tiered} argue that hot
data is not necessarily performance-critical, because memory-level parallelism
(MLP) can hide most of the slow-tier latency of some accesses but not of
others. They introduce the Amortized Offcore Latency (AOL) metric and build two
mechanisms on top of it: SOAR, an AOL-guided first-placement policy, and ALTO,
an AOL-based filter for page migrations. In this project we will reproduce the
main claims of the paper (hotness is a poor proxy of performance, AOL is a
better predictor of slowdown, SOAR and ALTO outperform state-of-the-art tiering
systems) on a traditional server and, if time permits, evaluate whether these
techniques also improve memory management on the PolyRack disaggregated rack
architecture~\cite{polyrack}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Why is the problem important?}
Server performance is directly tied to the number of instructions executed per
cycle: $\text{Perf} \propto \text{IPC} = 1/\text{CPI}$. Every cycle the core
spends stalled waiting for memory increases the CPI and therefore degrades
performance. Memory-intensive datacenter workloads (graph analytics, machine
learning, caching, HPC) keep growing in footprint, and provisioning all of
their memory in DRAM is expensive. Tiered memory architectures therefore add a
cheaper, higher-capacity slow tier (CXL memory, remote NUMA node) next to the
fast DRAM tier. The slow tier, however, is 2--3$\times$ slower than
DRAM~\cite{liu2025tiered}, so the way data is distributed across tiers
determines how many memory stalls the application suffers: placing too much
performance-critical data in the slow tier, or migrating pages too
aggressively, both translate into lost performance.

\subsection{What is involved in the problem?}
The problem involves (i) the two memory tiers and their latencies
$T_{\text{DRAM}}$ and $T_{\text{CXL}}$, (ii) a notion of data \emph{hotness},
i.e., how frequently a page or object is accessed, (iii) the \emph{initial
placement} policy that decides in which tier data is allocated, and (iv) the
\emph{migration} policy that promotes pages to the fast tier or demotes them to
the slow tier (and possibly re-migrates them) at run time.

A simple first-order model makes the dependencies explicit. Let $\alpha$ be the
fraction of data placed in DRAM. The average memory latency is
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{total}} = \alpha\,T_{\text{DRAM}} + (1-\alpha)\,T_{\text{CXL}}.
\end{equation}
Let $\beta_{\text{DRAM,hot}}$ ($\beta_{\text{CXL,hot}}$) be the fraction of
hot data in DRAM (CXL). Splitting each tier into hot and cold data gives
\begin{align}
T_{\text{total}} ={}& \underbrace{\alpha\,\beta_{\text{DRAM,hot}}T_{\text{DRAM}}}_{\text{hot in DRAM}}
 + \underbrace{\alpha\,(1-\beta_{\text{DRAM,hot}})T_{\text{DRAM}}}_{\text{cold in DRAM}} \nonumber\\
 &+ \underbrace{(1-\alpha)\,\beta_{\text{CXL,hot}}T_{\text{CXL}}}_{\text{hot in CXL}}
 + \underbrace{(1-\alpha)(1-\beta_{\text{CXL,hot}})T_{\text{CXL}}}_{\text{cold in CXL}}.
\end{align}
State-of-the-art (SOTA) tiering systems essentially try to move data from the
``hot in CXL'' term to the ``hot in DRAM'' term, i.e., to decrease
$\beta_{\text{CXL,hot}}$ and increase $\beta_{\text{DRAM,hot}}$.

\subsection{What causes the problem?}
Two factors cause poor tiering decisions. First, both slow-tier accesses and
page migrations are expensive (up to $\mu$s-scale for migrations), so wrong
decisions are costly. Second, and more fundamentally, the decisions are based
on hotness, which ignores \emph{memory-level parallelism}. Out-of-order cores
overlap independent memory requests, so the effective cost of an access is
roughly its latency divided by the degree of MLP. Let $\gamma_{\text{MLP,fast}}$
be the fraction of data accessed with high MLP (e.g., array traversals); the
remaining $1-\gamma_{\text{MLP,fast}}$ is accessed with low MLP (e.g.,
dependent pointer chasing). Writing $\beta = \beta_{\text{DRAM,hot}}$ and
$\gamma = \gamma_{\text{MLP,fast}}$, the DRAM part of the model becomes
\begin{align}
T_{\text{total}} ={}& \alpha\,\beta\,\gamma\,
  \frac{T_{\text{DRAM}}}{\text{MLP}_{\text{fast}}}
 + \alpha\,\beta\,(1-\gamma)
  \frac{T_{\text{DRAM}}}{\text{MLP}_{\text{slow}}} \nonumber\\
 &+ \alpha(1-\beta)\,\gamma\,
  \frac{T_{\text{DRAM}}}{\text{MLP}_{\text{fast}}}
 + \alpha(1-\beta)(1-\gamma)
  \frac{T_{\text{DRAM}}}{\text{MLP}_{\text{slow}}} \nonumber\\
 &+ \ldots
\end{align}
and analogously for the CXL terms. Hot data is often accessed with high MLP
(sequential scans), so its latency is already largely hidden even in the slow
tier, while ``colder'' but low-MLP data pays the full slow-tier latency. When a
hotness-based policy reduces $\beta_{\text{CXL,hot}}$, it fills DRAM with
high-MLP data and pushes low-MLP data to CXL: the low-MLP terms grow, CPI
increases, and performance drops. Hotness alone is therefore an unfaithful
proxy of performance.

\subsection{Does prior work solve the problem?}
No. SOTA tiering systems such as TPP, Nomad, Linux NUMA Balancing Tiering
(NBT) and Colloid all rely on hotness. On the paper's microbenchmark (one
sequential, high-MLP ``hot'' thread and one pointer-chasing, low-MLP ``cold''
thread), they underperform even a naive \emph{Cold-on-DRAM} placement, and
fall behind first-touch allocation without migrations (NoTier) by
12--14\%~\cite[Fig.~1]{liu2025tiered}. On real workloads such as
\texttt{bc-urand} (GAPBS betweenness centrality on a uniform random graph),
they can be up to 217\% slower than NoTier~\cite[Fig.~4]{liu2025tiered}.

\subsection{Overview of the proposed solution~\cite{liu2025tiered}}
\textbf{AOL.} The paper first shows (via an offline study of 56 workloads)
that the ratio of LLC stall cycles to total cycles, $P = s_{\text{LLC}}/c$, is
correlated with the slowdown on the slow tier, but overestimates it for
high-MLP workloads. It then defines $\text{AOL} = \text{Latency}/\text{MLP}$,
computed from offcore performance counters, and models the slowdown as
$S = P \times K$ with a correction factor $K = f(\text{AOL}) =
1/(a + b/\text{AOL})$. The constants $a,b$ depend only on the hardware and can
be calibrated with microbenchmarks; the Pearson correlation between predicted
and measured slowdown improves from 0.869 to 0.951.

\textbf{SOAR} (Static Object Allocation based on Ranking) profiles an
application, ranks objects by their AOL-weighted contribution to performance,
and places the highest-ranked objects in DRAM at allocation time. This yields
near-optimal first placement and removes the need for most later migrations.

\textbf{ALTO} observes that automatic hot-page migration often does not improve
performance and regulates migrations with the current AOL $l$
(Alg.~\ref{alg:alto}): below $\text{AOL}_{\text{low}}$ promotions are disabled,
above $\text{AOL}_{\text{high}}$ they are all enabled, and in between only a
fraction $F(l)$ is allowed. ALTO can be plugged into existing systems
(TPP, NBT, Nomad, Colloid).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ALTO: AOL-regulated page migrations (e.g., every 1\,s)~\cite{liu2025tiered}}
\label{alg:alto}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\text{AOL}_{\text{low}} \gets 40$, $\text{AOL}_{\text{high}} \gets 100$ \Comment{profiled offline}
\Ensure Adjusted page migration ratio \textit{Scale}
\State Profile the current AOL ($l$)
\If{$l \le \text{AOL}_{\text{low}}$}
  \State $\textit{Scale} \gets 0$ \Comment{no promotions}
\ElsIf{$l \ge \text{AOL}_{\text{high}}$}
  \State $\textit{Scale} \gets 1$ \Comment{all promotions}
\Else
  \State $\textit{Scale} \gets F(l)$ \Comment{partial promotions}
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Questions, Hypotheses and Tests}
\label{sec:questions}

The overarching question is: \emph{how can we improve server performance by
reducing effective memory latency, using metrics better than hotness and
building better data-placement procedures on top of them?} We break it down
into the following prioritized questions; their dependencies are summarized in
the hypothesis tree of Fig.~\ref{fig:tree}.

\subsection{Q1: Are hotness metrics really informative?}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Insight (I1):} MLP hides part of the memory latency, so simple
        hotness-based decisions lead to suboptimal system performance.
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis (H1):} hotness $\neq$ performance criticality.
  \item \textbf{Test (E1):} reproduce the hot/cold microbenchmark
        (sequential vs.\ pointer-chasing threads) and evaluate the performance
        of different placement strategies (All-on-DRAM, Hot-on-DRAM,
        Cold-on-DRAM, NoTier, SOTA systems), cf.\
        Fig.~1 of~\cite{liu2025tiered}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Q2: If not, how can a better metric be defined?}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Insight (I2):} ML/data-driven exploration of hardware
        counters across many workloads helps find which features actually
        explain slowdown.
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis (H2):} an ML-derived metric (AOL with correction
        factor $K$) is more representative of performance than hotness or
        raw LLC stalls.
  \item \textbf{Test (E2):} measure slowdown and counter features
        (LLC stalls/cycle, AOL) on a set of workloads, calibrate $K =
        f(\text{AOL})$, and compare the Pearson coefficient between measured
        slowdown and the predictors $P$ and $P\times K$, cf.\ Fig.~2
        of~\cite{liu2025tiered}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Q3: With this metric, can we improve first data placement (SOAR)?}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Insight (I3):} AOL improves first placement by correctly
        identifying performance-critical data.
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis (H3):} an AOL-based placement policy performs
        better than SOTA.
  \item \textbf{Test (E3):} compare the slowdown (w.r.t.\ All-on-DRAM) of SOAR
        vs.\ NoTier, Colloid, Nomad, NBT (and TPP) on different workloads and
        fast/slow-tier ratios, cf.\ Fig.~4 and Table~4
        of~\cite{liu2025tiered}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Q4: With this metric, can we optimize page migration policies (ALTO)?}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Insight (I4):} AOL allows filtering page migrations depending
        on their true performance criticality.
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis (H4):} SOTA + ALTO page filtering performs better
        than SOTA alone.
  \item \textbf{Test (E4):} compare slowdown, effective memory latency and
        number of page promotions of SOTA+ALTO vs.\ SOTA alone, cf.\ Fig.~7
        and Table~5 of~\cite{liu2025tiered}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Q5: Can these elements improve PolyRack?}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Insight (I5):} AOL, SOAR and ALTO increase performance on
        tiered-memory servers; PolyRack disaggregates memory into dedicated
        memory servers connected through a CXL-class fabric, which creates a
        similar fast/slow memory hierarchy.
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis (H5):} AOL/SOAR/ALTO also lead to better
        performance on PolyRack.
  \item \textbf{Test (E5):} compare slowdown and memory latency on PolyRack
        with vs.\ without AOL/SOAR/ALTO on different workloads, and against a
        traditional tiered server.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
We will first reproduce the results on a traditional server, and only then
move to PolyRack. Following~\cite{liu2025tiered}, if no real CXL device is
available the slow tier will be emulated with a remote NUMA node (lowering its
uncore frequency and disabling its cores, which in the paper yields 90/190\,ns
fast/slow latencies). We will use the open-source implementation of SOAR and
ALTO (\url{https://github.com/MoatLab/SoarAlto}), Linux \texttt{perf} and the
offcore/LLC performance counters needed to compute $P$ and AOL, and
\texttt{numactl}/cgroups to control the fast/slow-tier ratio.
\todo{Confirm server access, CPU/kernel version, CXL vs.\ NUMA emulation, and
whether experiments run on bare metal or in a VM.}

\subsection{Workloads}
We will start from \texttt{bc-urand} and other graph workloads from the GAP
Benchmark Suite (GAPBS), which stress tiered memory with irregular accesses,
plus the hot/cold microbenchmark of Q1. Time permitting, we will add workloads
from the other categories used in the paper (machine learning, caching, HPC).
\todo{Agree on the final workload list with the TA (and on PolyRack's home
workloads).}

\subsection{Baselines and metrics}
Baselines: All-on-DRAM (upper bound), NoTier (first-touch, no migrations),
and the hotness-based SOTA systems TPP, NBT, Nomad and Colloid.
Metrics: slowdown relative to All-on-DRAM (lower is better), Pearson
correlation between predicted and measured slowdown, AOL and average memory
latency, and migration traffic (number of page promotions/demotions), all
measured across several fast/slow-tier ratios (e.g., 10--90\% of the
workload's RSS in the slow tier).
